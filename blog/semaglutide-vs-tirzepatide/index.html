    Semaglutide vs. Tirzepatide: How the Two Leading GLP-1 Medications Compare

    Oakwell MD care team · October 15, 2026 · 6 min read

    Semaglutide and tirzepatide are the two names that come up first in almost every weight loss consult. Semaglutide is the active ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic, while tirzepatide is the active ingredient in Zepbound and Mounjaro. Each one is a single shot under the skin, once a week. They act on gut hormones that tell the brain you have eaten enough, but they are not interchangeable, and the differences show up in dosing and in how the stomach reacts.

    How each medication works

    Semaglutide copies GLP-1, a hormone your gut releases after a meal. GLP-1 slows stomach emptying, signals fullness to the brain and helps the pancreas release insulin when blood sugar rises, and the medication form stays active for roughly a week, while the natural hormone fades in minutes. That is why it is weekly. One pen, one day, every week.

    Tirzepatide activates the GLP-1 receptor plus a second one called GIP, another gut hormone involved in insulin release and how the body stores energy. That second target matters. The second receptor appears to strengthen the drop in appetite. Patients on either drug often describe less food noise, meaning fewer background thoughts about the next snack and an easier time stopping at a normal portion.

    What the clinical trials showed

    In the STEP 1 trial, adults without diabetes taking semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly lost about 15 percent of their body weight on average over 68 weeks, compared with roughly 2 percent on placebo. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide at the top dose of 15 mg produced an average loss of about 21 percent over 72 weeks. A later head-to-head study, SURMOUNT-5, found larger average losses with tirzepatide. Averages hide a lot. Some people lose far more than the mean and some lose less, so neither number predicts your result.

    Dosing, titration and side effects

    Both drugs start low and climb slowly. Wegovy begins at 0.25 mg weekly and steps up every four weeks to a 2.4 mg maintenance dose, which puts the full dose roughly four months away for most patients. Zepbound starts at 2.5 mg and rises in 2.5 mg steps every four weeks, up to a maximum of 15 mg. Your provider may hold you at a lower step longer if side effects flare.

    Nausea is the most common complaint with either medication, followed by diarrhea and constipation. In the trials, these effects were usually mild to moderate and tended to show up after a dose increase, then settle within several days as patients got used to the new dose. Some patients tolerate one drug better than the other. That is one reason a switch is sometimes on the table.

    Neither drug is for everyone. The labels carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodents, so they are not used in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2. They are also avoided in pregnancy, and a history of pancreatitis or gallbladder disease gets a closer look from the provider.

    How a provider helps you choose

    The choice is usually practical. A provider will ask about your weight history and whether you have tried a GLP-1 before. Pharmacy supply and insurance coverage also play a part, and both can change from one month to the next without much warning.

    • Tirzepatide had the higher average weight loss in head-to-head data.
    • Semaglutide has the longer track record, including a cardiovascular outcomes trial.
    • Semaglutide also comes in a daily tablet form, which some patients prefer.
    • Side effect patterns are similar, but tolerance differs from person to person.

    Switching between the two is common. A patient who stalls on semaglutide 2.4 mg, or who cannot tolerate it, may move to tirzepatide, usually starting at 2.5 mg or 5 mg depending on the provider's judgment rather than at a matched dose. Expect a fresh titration. Some stomach upset may return briefly after changing drugs.

    Weight is not the only outcome providers weigh. In the SELECT trial, semaglutide cut major cardiovascular events by roughly 20 percent in people with existing heart disease. Zepbound is also approved for obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. For some patients, that tips the decision.

    Ozempic and Mounjaro are FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes, not weight loss, even though they contain the same ingredients as Wegovy and Zepbound. Compounded versions of either drug are not FDA-approved and are prescribed only when a provider decides they are appropriate. Ask which one you are getting. Your pharmacy label will name it plainly.
